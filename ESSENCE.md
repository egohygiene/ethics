# Essence: Understand the System. Preserve the Person.

> **Status:** Living author's perspective · Philosophical foundation · Not an empirical finding  
> **Scope:** The values and questions motivating Ego Hygiene Ethics, not a claim that every AI application is beneficial or that every critic is mistaken.

## In one minute

A technology is not a moral personality. It is a set of methods embedded in systems of people, institutions, incentives, infrastructure, and consequences.

Real harms deserve real scrutiny. But identifying a harm is not, by itself, a justification for assigning moral guilt to every person using a broad class of tools.

We can defend creative freedom while demanding consent, environmental responsibility, worker dignity, public accountability, and safer engineering. These are not competing obligations that require choosing one and denying the rest.

**The question is not simply “Is AI good or bad?” It is: What is happening, to whom, through which mechanisms, compared with which alternatives, and what can we improve?**

## 1. The moral object matters

“AI” covers many different methods, applications, deployments, and consequences. Treating that label as a single ethical object compresses an entire ecosystem into two letters.

Distinguish at least:

- **Method:** mathematical models, algorithms, data representations, and computational techniques.
- **Workload:** the actual tasks, model architecture, hardware, software, and orchestration used.
- **Deployment:** infrastructure siting, electricity and water, procurement, labor, safety, and governance.
- **Use:** the intention, context, consent, competence, and effects of a particular application.
- **Institutions:** the companies, regulators, customers, communities, and people who can change the system.

Responsibility can be distributed across these layers. Distributed responsibility does not mean no one is responsible.

A community harmed by a data-center project deserves attention and remedies. An artist using an AI tool is not automatically answerable for every decision made by that provider. Neither proposition cancels the other.

The scale of consumption still matters: a Discord message and a generated video can both depend on digital infrastructure without having remotely equivalent footprints. Comparisons must measure impacts, not merely point out that both use computers.

## 2. Expertise is a practice of inquiry

Knowing a technology well does not inherently bias someone in its favor. Personal benefit, professional identity, financial interests, social pressures, and selected evidence **can** introduce bias; so can personal harm or opposition.

The useful test is not whether someone has a label such as “expert.” It is whether they can explain mechanisms, distinguish what they know from what they infer, test claims, acknowledge limits, and revise their views.

Engineering knowledge is valuable but does not replace lived community experience, law, ethics, labor expertise, or environmental science. Conversely, moral concern does not make technical details irrelevant.

**Competence does not require omniscience. Reliability can mean knowing how to find out.**

An informed person may still reasonably refuse AI tools. Criticism is not evidence of ignorance. What this project challenges is unjustified certainty and blanket personal condemnation, not disagreement itself.

## 3. Ethical judgment needs scope and proportion

A criticism should make clear **which practice, mechanism, company, policy, deployment, or outcome** it concerns. Better questions include:

- What is the specific harm or benefit, and what evidence supports it?
- Who is affected, including people not represented in the debate?
- Who has decision-making power, and which responsibilities are feasible at each level?
- What comparable alternatives exist, and which tradeoffs would change?
- What remains unknown or disputed?
- Which remedies can reduce harm without unnecessarily constraining other people's autonomy?

Rejecting a practice personally is legitimate. Arguing for broader restrictions may also be legitimate, but it requires an argument proportionate to the restriction. Publicly reducing another person to “bad” merely for using a category of technology is an especially weak substitute for that argument.

## 4. Creativity can move up an abstraction layer

For the author, generative tools are not simply output machines. They can become expressive interfaces to a vast space of possibilities: composing, steering, selecting, editing, sequencing, and combining outputs with years of accumulated craft and intention.

This can make larger-scale creative expression accessible to individuals who could not otherwise execute every part of a project alone. It can also cause genuine problems involving training consent, authorship, compensation, attribution, displacement, and concentration of power.

Manual and AI-assisted arts can coexist. Neither is inherently superior. The human significance of a creative process is not exhausted by the tools it employs, nor does personal meaning erase the rights of other contributors.

The purpose of creating art need not be monetization. Art can be a way to communicate what is hard to say aloud, make contact with other people, and give form to emotions or ideas that otherwise remain unexpressed.

## 5. Evaluate whole workflows, not slogans

An AI request may be energy-intensive. It may also replace a longer sequence of expensive work. Whether it **actually** reduces total energy depends on the backend and the comparison boundary.

The author's **Energy per Accepted Output** idea is a testable hypothesis: intentional planning, a still frame, and a refined prompt might avoid multiple video-generation retries. Added planning has a cost, too.

For comparable outcomes, compare the **entire workflow**, including preparation, compute, retries, human effort, supporting infrastructure, and measurement uncertainty.

Efficiency per successful output can improve while aggregate demand grows. Lower private cost does not automatically mean lower emissions. The appropriate response is instrumentation, transparency, experiments, and better system design—not a presumption of either salvation or catastrophe.

EcoLogits, CodeCarbon, standardized benchmarks, public energy data, and reproducible experiments are ways to investigate pieces of this question. None alone measures every lifecycle impact of a closed-model creative workflow.

## 6. Incentives distort what gets said

Money can support creative work, research, and infrastructure. It can also create incentives to optimize for engagement, fear, certainty, and constant public opinions rather than careful inquiry.

This is a concern about **systems of incentives**, not proof of any particular commentator's hidden motives. Criticism may be sincere and well founded; criticism may also be shallow, performative, or monetized. The content and evidence still need to be evaluated on their merits.

The author wants to build and create from curiosity and care, not orient every act of expression around what can be sold. Financial necessity is real, but meaning should not have to be converted into a product to count.

## 7. Anger can be expressed without becoming doctrine

Private venting, public judgment, and action are different things.

The author sometimes speaks with more anger and absolutism in private than reflects his considered beliefs. That expression can help identify fear, hurt, and violated values; it is not a reliable transcript of what he believes about whole groups of people.

The goal is not to suppress every harsh feeling or pretend to be perfectly regulated. It is to **translate emotion into specific, proportionate, and defensible action** before it becomes a public accusation.

This includes the right to be quiet, decline confrontation, and refuse to spend one's life arguing with provocateurs. Non-escalation is not weakness. Nor does rejecting someone else's generalization justify replacing it with a new one.

## 8. A person is more than an argument

People reason from different starting conditions: biology, history, education, safety, relationships, culture, and chance. Each of us grows from a different “seed,” with a partial view of the larger ecosystem.

The perspective of the individual and that of the collective are mutually influential. Individuals shape institutions; institutions shape the circumstances in which people think, cope, work, create, and disagree.

Recognizing this does not require approving harm or treating all claims as equally true. It does ask that we leave room for incomplete knowledge, development, accountability, and change.

This is the aspiration behind **psychological flexibility**: to hold beliefs seriously without making them untouchable extensions of identity.

## 9. What the observatory promises

Ego Hygiene Ethics should:

1. Separate observation, estimate, projection, interpretation, hypothesis, and author's perspective.
2. Trace consequential claims to inspectable sources and disclose gaps.
3. Give competing perspectives a fair hearing without manufactured equivalence.
4. Distinguish criticism of practices from moral labeling of people.
5. Show engineering responses and possible remedies alongside documented harms.
6. Evaluate both impact per useful outcome and total system impact.
7. Welcome corrections, including findings that challenge the author's own preferences.
8. Protect the reader's agency: **the dashboard helps people judge; it does not judge people for them.**

Its public visualizations and factual claims remain governed by [Epistemology](EPISTEMOLOGY.md), [Methodology](METHODOLOGY.md), and the documented evidence model. This essay is a statement of orientation, not evidence for a numerical claim.

## The essential distinction

**Understand before condemning. Measure before generalizing. Hold powerful institutions accountable without erasing individual humanity. Create without requiring everyone else's approval.**

A world made of different people needs both criticism and creation, both scrutiny and compassion. The point is not to win every argument. It is to leave the system—and the people living inside it—better than we found them.
